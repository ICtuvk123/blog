Pruning

MLPerf: A family of standardized benchmark suites developed by MLCommons for evaluating the performance of machine-learning systems. Depending on the suite, it may measure quantities such as training time, inference throughput, latency, accuracy, and power consumption. MLPerf is therefore a benchmark suite rather than a single hardware metric.

Embedded devices usually operate under strict energy, memory, and latency constraints. One important optimization target is data movement, especially accesses to off-chip memory. The exact energy cost depends on the hardware and memory hierarchy, but an off-chip DRAM access can consume orders of magnitude more energy than a simple arithmetic operation. Therefore, reducing the model size does not only reduce computation; it can also reduce expensive memory traffic.

As illustrated above, pruning introduces sparsity into a model by removing selected weights or larger structures. A common formulation is

$$ \min_{W_p} \mathcal{L}(W_p; \mathcal{D}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert W_p \rVert_0 \leq K, $$

where $W_p$ is the pruned weight tensor, $\mathcal{L}$ is the task loss, and $\lVert W_p \rVert_0$ denotes the number of nonzero parameters. Equivalently, pruning can be represented using a binary mask $M$:

$$ W_p = M \odot W, $$

where $M$ determines which parameters are retained.

The goal of pruning is usually to reduce model size, computation, memory traffic, or latency while preserving as much predictive accuracy as possible. Pruning does not generally improve accuracy by itself. In some experiments, the pruned model may achieve slightly better accuracy because sparsity acts as a form of regularization or because of the subsequent fine-tuning process, but this is not guaranteed.

A common pruning pipeline is:

  1. Train a dense model.
  1. Estimate the importance of weights, channels, filters, or other structures.
  1. Remove the least important components.
  1. Fine-tune the remaining parameters to recover lost accuracy.

Some methods instead learn the sparse structure jointly during training.

Fine-grained & Coarse-grained Pruning

Pruning methods can be broadly divided into fine-grained pruning and coarse-grained pruning.

Fine-grained pruning, also called unstructured pruning, removes individual weights. Because every weight can be selected independently, it provides considerable flexibility and can often reach a high sparsity level with relatively little accuracy loss. However, the resulting sparse matrix has an irregular pattern. Standard dense GPU kernels usually cannot convert this theoretical reduction in multiply-accumulate operations into proportional latency improvements. Efficient execution often requires specialized sparse kernels, compressed storage formats, or dedicated hardware, and the storage of indices introduces additional overhead.

Coarse-grained pruning, also called structured pruning, removes regular structures such as channels, filters, blocks, attention heads, or entire layers. In a matrix representation, this may correspond to deleting complete rows or columns. Structured pruning is less flexible and may remove some useful parameters together with unimportant ones, but it produces smaller dense tensors that can usually be accelerated directly by standard CPUs, GPUs, and deep-learning libraries.

Pruning Strategies

Magnitude-based Pruning

The most direct pruning strategy is magnitude-based pruning. Instead of pruning only weights that are already exactly zero, it assumes that weights with small absolute values have relatively little influence on the output and removes them according to

$$ \text{importance}(w_i) = |w_i|. $$

For unstructured pruning, individual weights with the smallest magnitudes are removed. For structured pruning, the importance of an entire group, such as a channel or filter, can be estimated using a group norm such as

$$ \text{importance}(W_g) = \lVert W_g \rVert_1 \quad \text{or} \quad \lVert W_g \rVert_2. $$

The groups with the smallest norms are then pruned. This is a useful heuristic, but a small norm does not always imply that the group is unimportant because the final effect also depends on the input activations and the surrounding layers.

Scaling-based Pruning

Another strategy assigns a learnable scaling factor to each channel or structural component. If the output of channel $i$ is $z_i$, the scaled activation can be written as

$$ \tilde{z}_i = \gamma_i z_i. $$

A channel whose scaling factor $|\gamma_i|$ is sufficiently small can be considered a pruning candidate. The scaling factor may be an explicitly introduced learnable gate or, in methods such as network slimming, the learnable $\gamma$ parameter of a batch-normalization layer.

To make these factors informative for pruning, the model is often trained with a sparsity-inducing regularizer such as an $L_1$ penalty:

$$

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \lambda \sum_i |\gamma_i|. $$

After training, channels with small scaling factors are removed, together with the corresponding filter and input-channel slices in adjacent layers. The pruned network is then usually fine-tuned. A small batch-normalization scaling factor should not automatically be treated as proof that a channel is unimportant unless the model has been trained with an appropriate pruning objective or its effect has been validated.

Selection of Neurons to Prune

Removing a neuron or channel also removes the corresponding row or column of parameters connected to it. Therefore, neuron pruning and structured weight pruning are two views of the same structural modification.

Activation statistics can be used to estimate neuron or channel importance. For example, a channel may be considered less important if its activation magnitude is consistently small over a representative dataset:

$$

\text{importance}(i)

\frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \lVert a_i^{(n)} \rVert. $$

For networks using ReLU, another possible statistic is the average percentage of zero activations. ReLU indeed produces many zeros, but a channel being zero for one input does not mean that it can be removed permanently. Static pruning decisions should be based on statistics collected over many representative samples and, ideally, on the observed effect of removing the channel.

In the example above, channels with a high average zero-activation ratio may be selected as pruning candidates. More precisely, we prune channels or neurons rather than an entire “activation layer.” After a channel is removed, the corresponding output filter in the current layer and the corresponding input-channel weights in the next layer must also be removed.

Regression-based Pruning

Regression-based channel pruning selects channels according to how well the original output can be reconstructed after pruning.

Suppose the output of a layer can be decomposed as

$$ Y = \sum_{i=1}^{C} X_i * W_i, $$

where $X_i$ is the $i$-th input channel, $W_i$ is the corresponding slice of the weight tensor, and $*$ denotes convolution or the corresponding linear operation.

A channel-selection variable $\beta_i$ can be introduced for each input channel:

$$

\hat{Y}

\sum_{i=1}^{C} \beta_i X_i * W_i. $$

The pruning objective is to retain only a limited number of channels while minimizing the reconstruction error:

$$ \min_{\beta, W} \left\lVert Y- \sum_{i=1}^{C} \beta_i X_i * W_i \right\rVert_F^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert \beta \rVert_0 \leq C'. $$

Because the $L_0$ constraint is difficult to optimize directly, it is commonly approximated using an $L_1$-regularized regression problem such as LASSO. The method then alternates between two steps:

  1. Channel selection: keep the weights fixed and use sparse regression to identify a subset of channels, producing many $\beta_i=0$.
  1. Weight reconstruction: keep the selected channels fixed and optimize the remaining weights using least squares so that the pruned layer reproduces the original output as closely as possible.

When channel $i$ is removed, the corresponding activation channel $X_i$ and weight slice $W_i$ are both deleted. The number of input channels and the amount of computation decrease, while the output dimension of the layer can remain unchanged. Finally, the whole network is usually fine-tuned to compensate for errors that accumulate across layers
